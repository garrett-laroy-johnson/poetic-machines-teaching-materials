UIC | ART 151 | Intro to Creative Coding for New Media Art

  • instructor: Dr. Grace Laroy Johnson (he/they)
  • email: glj@uic.edu
  • time: Tuesday && Thursday, 9:00-11:30am
  • term: Fall 2026
  • location: Online (Zoom link in Canvas)

This is a studio-based course that introduces students to computer programming with an emphasis on using creative practices to formulate new ideas. No previous programming experience is required. Students will develop a technical aptitude for generating dynamic and interactive visual projects, and through a socio-historical analysis of computation, art and philosophy, contextualize their projects with an understanding of how computation has augmented how we make meaning from our visual environment. Composing a series of projects throughout the semester, students will experiment and develop their own creative strategies, culminating in an exhibition in New Art City, a collaborative 3D online environment. Students will complete the class with a foundation of technical skills to pursue individual learning of more advanced programming, scripting, and web development. There are no prerequisites for this course.

The class will be taught as a series of lectures, discussions, tutorials, project work, and student critiques.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

  • Write p5.js sketches that use variables, conditionals, loops, functions, arrays, and randomness/noise to generate images and animation. (coding sketches; Projects 1–2)
  • Design a generative system with tunable parameters, and choose among and defend its outputs. (Project 1)
  • Compose in time and space through animation, interaction, and a shared 3D exhibition. (Projects 2–3)
  • Situate your work within the histories and politics of computational art, including questions of authorship, AI, and access. (readings, group presentation)
  • Give specific, usable critique and act on the critique you receive. (critiques)
  • Use documentation and community resources to keep learning on your own. (coding sketches)

COURSE MATERIALS

Textbook (free PDF on the course site): Lauren McCarthy, Casey Reas, and Ben Fry, Make: Getting Started with p5.js.

Readings: selected chapters from Winnie Soon and Geoff Cox, Aesthetic Programming: A Handbook of Software Studies (Open Humanities Press, 2020; open access), which pairs p5.js techniques with critical and cultural readings.

External Course Website: I host a website for all of my courses, which contains examples, links to video tutorials, lists of relevant functions and concepts by week, and artist screenings and explanations of the techniques we are learning.

Software (free, browser-based): the p5.js Web Editor and New Art City. No software purchase is required for this course.

Equipment: a laptop or desktop computer (phones and tablets will not work), a reliable internet connection, and Zoom. Camera use is required during critique and tutorials.

Every tutorial is recorded and posted to Canvas.

Coding Sketches (18%)

Most weeks you will complete a short coding sketch that practices that week's technique; each one is described on its week's page. Sketches are graded on effort: a complete, good-faith attempt earns full credit, even if it doesn't fully work. Bring your questions to class, where we share and discuss sketches each week.

Group Presentation (18%)

In groups of 5–6, you will present on an area of creative coding and digital art outside what we make in p5.js: generative art, sound and music, or games and simulation. The artists are listed in detail on the Canvas. Each student chooses one artist from the group's topic (artist lists are on the Group Presentation page) and presents for 4–5 minutes:

  • show one or two of the artist's works and explain how they relate to the topic, paying particular attention to how the artist uses code and other technology
  • read and discuss the artist's own description of the work
  • give your own response: what you see, how it makes you feel, what you think it's about
  • describe how your own interests intersect with the topic, and how you might pursue it further

Participation (10%)

As the UIC Department of Art describes, a studio course is built on active participation: making together, thinking together, and learning from one another. Participation means coming to class prepared, sharing your work in progress, asking questions, and engaging thoughtfully in critique of your classmates' work. You can participate by speaking or in the Zoom chat.

Late Work

Projects

You will earn the majority of your grade from your sustained and considered engagement with three projects. Each project will ask you to integrate fundamental technical and artistic creative coding techniques into a polished art work for a collective classroom critique. All projects are assessed with the course project rubric below; each prompt lists its own technical requirements.

Project 1: Generative Drawing Machine

Using p5*js, create a drawing machine. We are drawing on the legacy of artists working in the branch of creative coding called generative art, including artists such as Vera Molnar, Lillian Schwartz, Manfred Mohr, Joan Truckenbrod, Casey Reas, Rafaël Rozendaal, Zach Lieberman, and Sougwen Chung. Ron Eglash's research on African fractals shows how generative, rule-based design long predates the computer.

Your drawing machine is a compositional system built using the foundational technical elements of this semester.

For loops are a fundamental structure of creative code that allow us to repeat a process like drawing a shape, but vary its position or color. We do this by using the structuring variable inside the for loop inside of drawing functions like rect() or fill(). A standard and reliable way to use a for loop is to nest one for loop inside another to create a grid of shapes. This provides you with a regular structure into which you will introduce variation.

Global variables are declared variables at the top of your sketch which allow you to tune your drawing machine. Think of this like your artist control panel. These variables must be integrated into your code in the draw loop to create significant and meaningful visual changes in how your drawing machine operates. noiseScale will control the effect of the noise() function. spacing may control the distance in pixels between each shape. numShapes may control the number of shapes in each row and column of a nested for loop drawing a grid. sizeMin and sizeMax might work together with the random variable to assign different size values to a shape with a range (e.g. let w = random(sizeMin,sizeMax); circle(x,y,w);).

Randomness will use the random() and/or noise() function to introduce non-linear or unexpected variation into how your shapes are drawn. You may randomize the color palette by declaring a variable let r = random(100,150) and using that as the red value in fill (e.g. fill(r,100,0)). You may offset the x position of each shape in your grid by declaring a variable let xOffset = random(-10,10) and adding that to the x position of each shape your drawing machine draws (e.g. circle(x+xOffset,y,w)).

Once you've gotten your drawing machine up and running, tune your drawing machine by experimenting with the global variables you've created. You may be inspired to go back and add other elements to your machine.

Ultimately, you will decide on a single image your drawing machine has created. Use randomSeed() and/or noiseSeed to "lock in" those. The idea here is to challenge yourself to make more and more discerning artistic decisions.

requirements

  • use at least one for loop. Some A-level projects may use only one, but it's very likely you will need to use two or more for loops, nested or otherwise.
  • use at least 4 global variables that can be changed to influence the outcome of your generative drawing machine
  • must be a static image. Use noLoop(). In other words, no animation.
  • use random() and/or noise()
  • use randomSeed() or noiseSeed() and select one final version of your compositions
  • you are present for critique and participate in others' critique
  • no WEBGL or 3D.

submission.

Submit both your p5.js URL as well as your exported image using save() at the very bottom of your draw loop. Note, if you use save without noLoop(), p5 will try to export images every frame of video!

Project 2: Animated Tableaus // Narrative, or Generative Art

We are now composing in time. Using p5*js, use the canvas to create an animated artwork. You may make a gamelike animation, a sketch of a scene of an animated narrative, or a moving composition that continues in the aesthetic of generative art.

Narrative "Tableau"

When we hear the word "animation", we might think of a scene with a background and foreground which either plays automatically (more scene or narrative adjacent) or is activated by interactivity (more game like).

You should not aim to create an entire narrative or playable game with win and lose conditions. This is what is meant by "tableau". Aim instead to create something that gestures towards that: in the case of the narrative scene, maybe you are setting the scene just before the story starts. In the case of the interactive element, you may aim to have a convincing and satisfying interactive behavior that moves a character along.

Generative Art Moving "Tableau"

You may elect to continue working in a more geometric and visually abstract fashion as we saw in the first project, except this time your work will require motion. This time you should not use noLoop() as we want for your composition to change over time. In this approach, you may use any and all of the techniques described above to create movement, including interactivity, changing variables linearly (+,-, for example), animation loops, LFOs, sine wave or circular movement, translate, rotate, etc. The difference is primarily that you are not trying to render a scene, but working with shapes (or images!) to create a more abstract visual.

In our tutorials, we have seen two different ways of approaching animation and movement in p5. Many of you will find it convenient to choose one or the other, but both approaches can be combined in interesting ways as well.

In both cases, you should aim to use coding elements which support your artistic vision, integrating elements from this module with the previous module. You do not need to use all of these, but you will likely use three or four from each module. What I am most interested in is that you have created a compelling animated sketch that evidences two weeks of work.

requirements

  • your sketch must be animated: do not use noLoop(); the composition changes over time
  • choose a narrative / game-like tableau or a generative moving tableau
  • use at least three techniques from Module 1 and at least three from Module 2 (reviewed below)
  • the work should evidence two weeks of development
  • you are present for critique and participate in others' critique

submission

Submit your p5.js editor URL and a short screen recording (15–30 seconds) of the sketch running. Projects are presented for critique in Week 11.

As a review:

Module 1: Generative art

  • variables (in both local and global scope)
  • simple 2D shapes
  • complex shapes with beginShape(), endShape(), and vertex()
  • for loops -- index style (where the for loop variable increments by one) -- drawing element style (where the for loop variable increments by some amount that is useful to a drawing operation)
  • nested for loops
  • random()
  • noise()
  • if/else conditional statements

Module 2: Animation and Movement

  • changing variables with +=, ++, etc.
  • using noise() with frameCount to fluidly change the output
  • opacity/transparency (either in background or stroke/fill to create different effects)
  • images -- textured images with tint() and filter() -- sprite animations or characters
  • transformations -- translate() -- rotate() -- scale()
  • sinusoidal movement -- drawing in a circular fashion with sin() and cos() -- drawing in a spiral fashion with sin() and cos() and a changing radius -- moving elements in a sine wave fashion with sin() and a changing angle input
  • easing -- smooth changes in position to create a more naturalistic movement (e.g. let easing = 0.01; let x = 100; let targetX = 200; let dx= targetX - x; x += dx * easing;)
  • animation loops -- if/else loops (e.g. loop from 0 to 255 and back let x = 0; x++; if (x>255){x=0;}) -- if/else loops with "bounce" (e.g. 0->255->0);
  • "LFO" (low frequency oscillator) modulation of animation loops (a slower loop that modifies the speed of the first loop)
  • interactivity -- from keyboard input (key(), function keyPressed()) -- from mouse input (mouseX,mouseY, function mousePressed())
  • arrays

Project 3: Immersive Virtual Exhibition

In this project, we will bring our p5 compositions into the artist-built platform New Art City, an online 3D virtual reality environment. Now, we are not only in time, but in 3D space. Each student will have their own space in our collaborative world. In your space, you are tasked with designing an environment for your work to be viewed. You will need to produce at least one new p5 sketch that is displayed in your space.

Considerations

How can you make use of this 3D space? Will your space be everyday or fantastical? Will it resemble a gallery, or be otherworldly? How can you use sound or images in order to create a sense of immersion?

Requirements

  • You must design your own 3D space using your own assets.
  • You can use as many p5 sketches you've made this semester as you like, but you must use at least one new p5 sketch as an asset in your space.
  • This new sketch should be a star in your show. It may be a static image, a moving image, or wrapped around a 3D object.
  • You may choose to explore "Miss Sammi's Garden" to find ready-made 3D assets you can import into your space.
  • You may use sound to create atmosphere in your space.
  • Submit a 100–200 word proposal in Week 14 describing your artistic intent and the coding techniques you plan to use.

Submission

Submit the link to your space in our New Art City world, the p5.js editor URL of your new sketch, and two or three screenshots of your space. We tour and critique the exhibition together in Week 15.

Generative AI

This class focuses on the craft of creative coding. A central purpose of the course is to develop an interplay between your computational thinking and aesthetic discernment. While it is possible to create aesthetically interesting works with generative AI, using AI to create your code does not support this goal. As such, no generative AI is allowed in the generation of code for this course. I will assess your understanding of the code as well as the aesthetic result.

You may use AI tools the way you would use documentation or a tutor: to help you understand a concept or an error message. The code and patches you submit should be your own work, and you should be able to explain them. In critique, you will walk us through your work line by line (or, in Max, object by object). If you can’t explain part of your work yet, bring it to office hours and we’ll work through it together.

Attendance

The following has been carefully written by the faculty of the UIC Art Department and is used in all art classes.

This is a studio course built on active participation, making together, thinking together, and learning from one another. If you are not in class, then you cannot participate. Therefore, students are expected to attend all scheduled classes for the full duration of the course. Three unexcused absences will lower your final grade by one full letter. Excessive absences would be considered four or more and may be cause for failure in the course. Two tardies, or leaving class early, or stepping out for long durations during class time (20 minutes or more) constitutes one absence. Any absence beyond two, that is not an excused absence, will affect your grade. Excused absences are only missed classes due to the death of an immediate family member, observation of a religious holiday, hospitalization, contagious sickness, or a victim of a crime, all of which should be accompanied by appropriate documentation provided to your professor within a week of the absence. All other reasons for missing class are considered unexcused.

*Please note that if you communicate with your professor in advance about a class to which you will be absent or tardy, that does not mean the absence or tardy is excused. Only the reasons written above, with documentation, yield an absence as excused. Also, if you have a friend in the same class who is, on your behalf, letting your professor know that you are running late, you will still be marked tardy.

*Please also note that it is your responsibility to track your attendance, to remain aware of it, and to communicate with your professor if you become concerned. Your professor will be taking attendance every class period, but it is not their obligation to communicate with you if your grade is being affected by your attendance.
