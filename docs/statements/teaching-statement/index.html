Teaching Statement

Grace Laroy Johnson, PhD · Application for Assistant Teaching Professor of Sound and Media Art, UC Santa Cruz

Teaching and Mentoring Philosophy

I was called to be a teacher in middle school when I first felt the classroom's unique ability to activate the collective interplay of ideas through expression. Expression takes many forms across varied temporalities: a student's question, an artistic performance, a half-working sketch shown to peers. I have elaborated that motivation into classroom strategies through my individual and collective study of the relational process philosophy of Alfred North Whitehead and related thinkers such as Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, through which I orient the tenets of evidence-based learning toward situated artistic practice. In his writings on education, Whitehead values both culture, “activity of thought, and receptiveness to beauty and humane feeling,” and specialized technique. He warns educators against “inert ideas,” those “merely received into the mind without being utilised, or tested, or thrown into fresh combinations.” A for loop or an oscillator stays inert until a student throws it into fresh combinations. As a teacher, I see my role as keeping students' practices in motion, toward three connected goals, each of which I assess directly.

Technique. Students develop facility with the foundational technics of computational media: iteration, variables, conditionals, functions and objects, oscillators, envelopes, sampling, and signal flow. These are like scales and études in musical performance, which are never practiced for their own sake, but for bringing into expressive movement. I teach these techniques with methods grounded in learning research. I always live-code my tutorials, thinking aloud with students as I go. I emphasize the puzzle-like logic of each technique, talking it through as we construct a for loop together and making and fixing errors in real time. I model interactively, asking rather than telling. Before I run any code, students predict what it will do, the first step of the PRIMM approach (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make), and then we ask why. Every abstract structure also gets a physical or embodied model: students act out a nested for loop's instructions together, or trace a signal path on the whiteboard before patching it in Max. Weekly coding sketches, our études, are graded on effort; they give students frequent, low-stakes practice and give me formative evidence that shapes the next class.

Discernment. These constructions bear potentials beyond puzzle-solving, which students realize in long-term projects. Students take the techniques from their coding sketches and tease out their aesthetic potential: a simple row of circles in p5.js may become compositionally important when a student maps their size or color to linear iteration or a random process. I provide many code examples that put the same technique toward different outcomes, and my project briefs set up constrained compositional systems in which students loop between experimentation and perceptive aesthetic discernment. In the first project of my Introduction to Creative Coding course, students build and tune a “generative drawing machine” from loops, at least four parameterizable global variables (their “control panel”), and seeded randomness. During studio time I meet with each student one-on-one to help them see experimental and aesthetic possibilities through targeted feedback. Ultimately, I strive to shepherd students toward developing their own processes for bringing these circuits into ordered systems for performance, composition, and installation.

Technicity. Students develop a critically informed practice for understanding how their expressions resonate in a broader social and cultural field. I pair technical units with listening sessions and screenings of historical and contemporary artists. I try to show the broad range of genres and aesthetics a single technique can serve: sampling and sequencing in early tape music and in Chicago house, or object-oriented programming in an art game and in a generative artwork. Students encounter technology as culturally inflected rather than neutral, which opens a space for them to consider how, and to what ends, they wish to inflect it themselves. In participatory critique, students explore these inflections by situating their own work in the context of other artists and cultural topics.

My assessments follow from these goals. The published rubric for all projects in my creative coding and interactive sound courses at UIC weights technical execution at 30% and experimentation at 20% (Technique), aesthetic discernment at 30% (Discernment), and critique at 20%, where students explain their choices and give specific feedback to their peers. Group research presentations on artists and traditions, along with discussion of readings and listening sessions, assess Technicity. When we discuss the rubric together in class, I emphasize that it rewards iteration and risk-taking as well as polish.

My policy on generative AI follows the same goals. Because my courses build an interplay between computational thinking and aesthetic discernment, students may not use generative AI to write their code or patches, but they may use it as they would documentation or a tutor, to understand a concept or an error message. I assess understanding as well as output. In critique, students walk us through their work line by line, or object by object in Max. If a student cannot yet explain part of their work, we meet during my office hours to work through it together. Explaining their own work builds the computational thinking students need to keep learning on their own. We also study AI systems as cultural technologies with histories, labor, and politics, an approach informed by my work on SAIC's Center for Teaching and Learning AI Working Group (2025).

My mentoring follows the same logic as my teaching. We begin where a student's practice already is: video game music, horror movie soundtracks, an obsession with moiré patterns, a critique of AI. In critique, one-on-one meetings, and studio visits, I connect it to the techniques and cultural contexts that can move it forward, and we talk through the “hidden curriculum” of artistic and academic professional life.

Teaching Experience and Effectiveness

I have taught sound and media art as an instructor of record since 2019, building on years as a teaching assistant in music history, digital sound, and media arts. At Arizona State University (ASU), SUNY Purchase, the School of the Art Institute of Chicago (SAIC), and the University of Illinois Chicago (UIC), I have designed and taught 34 sections of fifteen courses, from 100-level studios to graduate seminars. They range across creative coding (p5.js), interactive sound and DIY hardware for sound performance and installation (Max/MSP, Daisy), physical computing (Arduino), web art and electronic literature, motion graphics, and seminars on media philosophy. I teach in studio, tutorial, seminar, and lecture formats. I have taught ten online sections at SAIC, UIC, and SUNY Purchase, eight of them in creative coding, and this fall I am teaching Introduction to Interactive Sound at UIC as a hybrid course.

At ASU, I taught four sections of AME 111: Introduction to Digital Culture. In this 100-seat lecture-studio, students made artistic responses to surveillance, the mediated body, and the attention economy in Max/MSP, p5.js, and HTML/CSS, and the course earned me ASU's peer-reviewed Teaching Excellence Award in 2020. My UIC course Introduction to Creative Coding for New Media Art, taught online over Zoom, scaffolds three projects that each build on the last: a static generative drawing machine, an animated or interactive “tableau,” and an exhibition of p5.js creations in New Art City, a 3D web platform where each student designs a space within our shared world. My sound courses move from synthesis fundamentals (AM/FM, additive and subtractive synthesis, envelopes, sequencing) to real-time input, sampling, granular synthesis, and gestural control with game controllers and computer vision, ending in performances, DIY instruments, and generative sound sculptures and installations.

I also teach in the community: a four-part course on sound programming with Max's gen~ environment at Chicago's Experimental Sound Studio (2025), soldering and DIY synthesizer workshops in Chicago and Philadelphia (2024), and workshops on musicality with Estuary, the reprogrammable Eurorack synthesizer modules I designed for CCAM (Center for Concrete and Abstract Machines), the artist-run nonprofit I founded and direct in Chicago. Estuary workshops have taken place in Chicago (2025) and at Indiana University Bloomington's Eskenazi Museum of Art (2026), with more upcoming at Public Space One in Iowa City (November 2026) and Iowa State University's Graduate Design Studio (January 2027).

Student evaluations show consistent effectiveness across levels and formats. At UIC, students rated my overall teaching effectiveness 4.8 out of 5 (Fall 2024) and 4.67 out of 5 (Fall 2025, online). At SAIC, respondents in my intermediate creative coding course (Fall 2022, online) and my graduate seminar (Fall 2025) gave unanimous scores of 10 out of 10 for fostering a respectful, inclusive, and equitable learning environment and for engaging a range of perspectives. One UIC student wrote that the course “opened me up to new creative possibilities and it helped me get over my anxieties of coding.” A graduate student wrote, “This class genuinely changed the way I think and make artwork.”

At UC Santa Cruz, I am prepared to teach across the Creative Technologies curriculum: the creative coding sequence (CT 20 and CT 120), Sensors, Signals, and Interaction (CT 167I), the Sound and Listening Seminar (CT 167S), Machine Collaboration (CT 151), the Collaborative Production Practicum (CT 125), and the Senior Studio (CT 195). In Music, I am prepared to teach courses such as Electronic Music: Synthesis & Composition (MUSC 123A–C) and the Live Electroacoustic Music Ensemble (MUSC 129), where I would draw on my years performing with and directing the laptop orchestra LORKAS. I would bring open-source hardware for sound, including the Estuary instruments, into Sensors, Signals, and Interaction. Because Creative Technologies is an online major, every course I teach there would draw on what I have refined across ten online sections: live-coded tutorials recorded for later review, several channels for participation, and free, browser-based tools. I look forward to joining the Teaching and Learning Center's Teaching Professors Learning Community and to sharing these online and access-centered practices with colleagues, as I have done as an invited speaker at SAIC's Center for Teaching and Learning (2025).

Mentoring

I mentor students formally and informally, from first-year undergraduates to MFA candidates. Since 2021, I have held more than thirty individual studio visits with graduate students, in my seminar, across SAIC's Art and Technology / Sound Practices department, and beyond. One student wrote in a course evaluation that these visits make “the course feel grounded in your actual practice and not in just theory.” I am advising an MFA student on the literature review for a disability studies thesis on the aesthetics and perception of kinetic art. In a one-semester independent study in 2024, I guided an undergraduate through designing her own research study, a mixed-methods proposal on how non-realistic environments shape communication in social VR, with a literature review, interview and focus-group protocols, and an observational study. I have also served on the department's graduate admissions committee (2023–2024), chaired the Doctoral Consortium of the International Conference on Movement and Computing (MOCO) in 2019, and directed ASU's Arts, Media and Engineering Graduate Student Association (2015–2018).

Since 2019, I have written more than forty letters of recommendation. Students I have recommended have gone on to graduate study, including Sao Otake in the master's program at NYU's Interactive Telecommunications Program (ITP) and Jude Silvertree, who is pursuing an MLIS focused on digital media at the University of Pittsburgh.

These relationships have long tails. Leonardo Gabriel do Amaral developed his interest in metamodernism through studio visits and assignments in my graduate seminar, where we connected it to our reading of Amy Ireland and Maya B. Kronic's Cute Accelerationism. I then worked with him on the essay that became “Reference vs Reverence: Post-hyperpop & the Rebirth of the Future” (CLOT Magazine, 2026). Lorenzo Osterheim took the same seminar, then served as my teaching assistant in Art and Technology Practices and in the seminar itself. In Spring 2025, I hired him as an artist assistant for CCAM's exhibition How to See in the Dark. He and Ben Glass, also my former student, organized Ouroborotica, a group show for their MFA theses that expanded on topics from my seminar. The two went on to found Flatline Gallery, which takes its name from Mark Fisher's dissertation Flatline Constructs, discussed in the seminar. Through their curation, they continue to explore questions from the seminar and share them with Chicago's broader art community.

I also mentor in the community. A student from my gen~ course at Experimental Sound Studio now develops and releases his own Max for Live devices built with gen~, and I am currently advising him on PCB and firmware design for a guitar pedal based on the Daisy Seed. When I created a YouTube series introducing gen~, I commissioned him to compose its opening and closing music with instruments he designed.

Through CCAM, I extend this mentoring beyond the classroom. I commission artists to design and lead public workshops, and I mentor them through feedback on curriculum and pedagogy. Workshop leaders have included my former students Juliana Castro Duperly and Patrick Glennon. Another former student, Juan Flores, worked with me to develop firmware for CCAM Earth, a reprogrammable desktop synthesizer I developed, for our May 2024 program Futurhythmachines: Chicago House Music; all three were paid for their work. Many other former students have joined CCAM's gen~ working group. CCAM also sells Estuary modules at workshops and at Experimental Sound Studio's experimental garage sale, and I work with community members as they develop their own firmware for them.

My mentoring aims to help students develop structures that support healthy professional practices and relationships, with attention to the work, family, and financial pressures many students carry. My mentees and I set shared expectations at the outset, meet on a regular schedule, and develop a timetable for the project they are working on. I make the hidden curriculum explicit by teaching students how to document their work, write artist statements, prepare graduate school applications, respond to open calls, and apply for grants. I encourage students to build a network of mentors to complement my singular perspective. When possible, I turn mentoring into paid professional experience: as a teaching assistant, artist assistant, collaborator, or workshop leader. At UCSC, I would mentor Creative Technologies students through the Senior Studio (CT 195), supervise independent studies in sound and creative coding, and help students present their work on campus and beyond, including at conferences such as NIME and MOCO. I would also seek out formal mentor training to deepen this practice.

Teaching and Mentoring Students from a Range of Social, Cultural, and Educational Backgrounds

Teaching at three public universities, I have served students for whom the intersection of art and technology was not an obvious destination. Like UCSC, ASU and UIC are Hispanic-Serving Institutions, and about four in ten students at SUNY Purchase are the first in their families to attend college. These schools enroll many low-income and first-generation students. In my teaching, I have adopted a principle from ASU's charter: success is measured “not by whom it excludes, but by whom it includes and how they succeed.”

My Introduction to Creative Coding at UIC fulfills a general education requirement. In Fall 2025, 78% of responding students took it for that reason; most came from engineering and from liberal arts and sciences, with others from nursing, medicine, the honors college, and the arts, and 44% were in their first year. They rated the course 4.67 out of 5 for being intellectually challenging and stimulating and 4.56 for increasing their interest in the subject. Four commitments guide my teaching across these differences.

Lowering Costs. My introductory courses assume no prior programming and are built on free, browser-based tools such as the open-source p5.js and the New Art City platform, so no student has to buy software or a powerful computer to participate. At UIC, I use department-instituted course fees to pay for physical computing kits and Max/MSP licenses.

Designing for Neurodiversity and Disability. My approach to access is grounded in research and in collaboration with educators and learning scientists. I co-founded and worked as a researcher on a National Science Foundation–funded research–practice partnership (2021–2025) that developed tangible, musical computational thinking activities with teachers of neurodiverse middle school students. I have co-authored research on neurodiversity and schooling (Reconceptualizing Educational Research Methodology, 2022) and co-presented on wearable technologies for sensory regulation at the Arizona Council on Exceptional Children (2022). Following the philosopher and artist Erin Manning, who extends Whitehead's process philosophy to neurodiversity, I treat differences in attention, movement, and perception as resources for art-making rather than as lack to be accommodated. In my teaching practice, I present every concept in several modes (live demonstration, recording, annotated example code available online, textbook selections, YouTube tutorial links, and embodied activity). I also offer several ways to participate, including speaking and chat. UIC students rated my sensitivity to “cultural/human diversity, diverse worldviews, learning disability, and/or physical disability” 5.0 (Fall 2024) and 4.88 (Fall 2025) out of 5.

Creating Belonging Through Considered Inclusion. I teach technology as culturally inflected, pairing canonical figures in new media art with artists and traditions its histories often leave out. In my graduate seminar, students build a collaborative wiki that maps the course's concepts and connects them to cultures and ideas they already know, so that each student's background becomes part of the class's shared knowledge. SAIC's Center for Teaching and Learning funded the wiki and invited me to present its design in 2025. A graduate student wrote that when I brought the Chinese science fiction writer Liu Cixin into a seminar discussion, it “resonated with me and made me feel very happy,” noting that instructors in other courses sometimes overlook Chinese artists. Through CCAM, where I approach programming not as mere event planning but as the creative design of learning communities, I have co-organized three annual editions (2024–2026) of We Don't Know Yet: What a Cinema Can Do, an expanded cinema night with the Onion City Film Festival that centers BIPOC and queer artists, and Futurhythmachines: Chicago House Music (2024), a program of panels, workshops, and performances.

Flexible Structures and Accountability. My classroom offers flexibility with accountability. I record every tutorial, in person and online, and post it to the course site, and students consistently name the recordings as essential (“The ZOOM recordings helped me a lot throughout the course”). Late coding sketches earn half credit for a week after their module ends, and late projects lose one letter grade per week rather than being refused. My project prompts list their requirements, considerations, and submission instructions alongside the published rubric, so expectations never depend on knowing unwritten rules. I also revise my courses in response to students: when a Fall 2022 student explained that having beginners teach programming fundamentals to one another early in the term caused confusion, I began opening with instructor-led, think-aloud tutorials and moved student-led teaching to group presentations later in the term. At UCSC, I would work with the Teaching and Learning Center to examine my course outcomes by student group and use anonymous mid-quarter check-ins to catch problems while they can still be fixed.
