## Teaching and Mentoring Philosophy

I was called to be a teacher in middle school, when I first felt the classroom's unique ability to activate the collective interplay of ideas through active expression. Expression takes many forms, of varied temporalities: a student's question, an artistic performance, a half-working sketch shown to peers. I have elaborated that motivation into classroom strategies through my individual and collective study of the relational process philosophy of Alfred North Whitehead and related thinkers such as Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, which lets me orient the tenets of evidence-based learning toward situated artistic practice. In his writings on education, Whitehead values both culture, “activity of thought, and receptiveness to beauty and humane feeling,” and specialized technique, and he guards against “inert ideas,” those “merely received into the mind without being utilised, or tested, or thrown into fresh combinations.” A for loop or an oscillator stays inert until a student throws it into fresh combinations. I see my role as keeping these practices in motion, toward three connected goals, each of which I assess directly.

**Technique.** Students develop facility with the foundational technics of computational media: iteration, variables, conditionals, functions and objects, oscillators, envelopes, and signal flow. These are like scales and études in musical performance, or the strength training that supports an athlete's dynamic movement: never practiced for their own sake, but for bringing into movement. I teach them with methods grounded in learning research. In live, think-aloud tutorials, I emphasize the puzzle-like logic of each technique, talking it through as we construct a for loop together, and making and fixing errors in real time. I model interactively, asking rather than telling. Before I run any code, students predict what it will do, the first step of the PRIMM approach (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make), and then we ask why. Every abstract structure also gets a physical or embodied model: students act out a nested for loop's instructions together, or trace a signal path on the whiteboard before patching it in Max. Weekly coding sketches, our études, are graded on effort; they give students frequent, low-stakes practice and give me formative evidence that shapes the next class.

**Discernment.** These constructions bear potentials beyond puzzle-solving. In sound and media art, a technique is already in movement, generating aesthetic stimulus: a simple row of circles in p5.js becomes a composition once a student maps iteration to size or color. I provide many code examples that put the same technique toward different outcomes, and my project briefs set up constrained compositional systems in which students loop between experimentation and judgment. One example is a generative “drawing machine” built from loops, at least four tunable global variables, and seeded randomness. During studio time I meet with each student one-on-one to recognize experimental possibilities, offer targeted feedback, and talk through aesthetic decisions. Ultimately, I want students to develop their own processes for bringing these circuits into ordered systems for performance, composition, and installation.

**Technicity.** Students develop an analytically informed practice for understanding how their expressions resonate in a broader social and cultural field. I pair technical units with listening sessions and screenings of historical and contemporary artists, and I try to show the full range of genres and aesthetics a single technique can serve: sampling and sequencing in early tape music and in Chicago house, or object-oriented programming in an art game and in a generative artwork. Students meet technology as culturally plural rather than neutral, and in participatory critique they practice situating artworks, including their own, in cultural and aesthetic discourse.

My assessments follow from these goals. One published rubric, shared by every project in a course, weights technical execution at 30% (Technique), experimentation and range at 20% and aesthetic discernment at 30% (Discernment), and critique at 20%, where students explain their choices and give specific feedback to their peers. Group research presentations on artists and traditions, along with discussion of readings and listening sessions, assess Technicity. Because students see in advance what each grade looks like, the rubric rewards iteration and risk-taking alongside polish.

My policy on generative AI follows the same goals. Because my courses build an interplay between computational thinking and aesthetic discernment, students may not use generative AI to write their code or patches, but they may use it as they would documentation or a tutor, to understand a concept or an error message. I assess understanding as well as output. In critique, students walk us through their work line by line, or object by object in Max, and anything they cannot yet explain becomes our agenda in office hours. Explaining their own work builds the metacognition students need to keep learning on their own. We also study AI systems as cultural technologies with histories, labor, and politics, an approach informed by my work on SAIC's Center for Teaching and Learning AI Working Group (2025). My mentoring follows the same logic as my teaching. I begin where a student's practice already is, connect it to the readings, tools, and people that can move it forward, and make the unwritten rules of artistic and academic life explicit.


## Teaching Experience and Effectiveness

I have taught sound and media art as an instructor of record since 2019, building on years as a teaching assistant in music history, digital sound, and media arts. At Arizona State University, SUNY Purchase, the School of the Art Institute of Chicago (SAIC), and the University of Illinois Chicago (UIC), I have designed and taught 34 sections of fifteen courses, from 100-level studios to graduate seminars. They range across creative coding (p5.js), physical computing (Arduino), interactive sound and DIY hardware for sound performance and installation (Max/MSP, Daisy), web art and electronic literature, motion graphics, and seminars on media philosophy. I teach in every format. I have taught ten online sections at SAIC, UIC, and SUNY Purchase, eight of them in creative coding, and this fall I am teaching Introduction to Interactive Sound at UIC as a hybrid course.

At ASU, I taught four sections of AME 111: Introduction to Digital Culture. In this 100-seat lecture-studio, students made artistic responses to surveillance, the mediated body, and the attention economy in Max/MSP, p5.js, and HTML/CSS, and the course earned me ASU's peer-reviewed Teaching Excellence Award in 2020. My UIC course Introduction to Creative Coding for New Media Art, taught online over Zoom, scaffolds three projects that each build on the last: a static generative drawing machine, an animated or interactive “tableau,” and an exhibition in New Art City, a 3D web platform where each student designs a space within our shared world. My sound courses move from synthesis fundamentals (AM/FM, additive and subtractive synthesis, envelopes, sequencing) to sampling, granular synthesis, and gestural control with game controllers and computer vision, ending in a performance, sound sculpture, or installation. I also teach in the community: a four-part course on sound programming with Max's gen~ environment at Chicago's Experimental Sound Studio (2025); soldering and DIY synthesizer workshops in Chicago and Philadelphia (2024); and, across the Midwest, workshops on musicality with Estuary, the reprogrammable Eurorack synthesizer modules I designed for CCAM, in Chicago (2025) and at Indiana University Bloomington's Eskenazi Museum of Art (2026). Upcoming Estuary workshops will take place at Public Space One in Iowa City (November 2026) and Iowa State University's Graduate Design Studio (January 2027).

Student evaluations show consistent effectiveness across levels and formats. At UIC, students rated my overall teaching effectiveness 4.8 out of 5 (Fall 2024) and 4.67 out of 5 (Fall 2025, online). At SAIC, respondents in my intermediate creative coding course (Fall 2022, taught primarily online) and my graduate seminar (Fall 2025) gave unanimous scores of 10 out of 10 for fostering a respectful, inclusive, and equitable learning environment and for engaging a range of perspectives. One UIC student wrote that the course “opened me up to new creative possibilities and it helped me get over my anxieties of coding.” A graduate student wrote, “This class genuinely changed the way I think and make artwork.”

At UC Santa Cruz, I am prepared to teach across the Creative Technologies curriculum: the creative coding sequence (CT 20 and CT 120), Sensors, Signals, and Interaction (CT 167I), the Sound and Listening Seminar (CT 167S), Machine Collaboration (CT 151), the Collaborative Production Practicum (CT 125), and the Senior Studio (CT 195). In Music, I am prepared to teach courses such as Electronic Music: Synthesis & Composition (MUSC 123A–C) and the Live Electroacoustic Music Ensemble (MUSC 129), where I would draw on my years directing the laptop orchestra LORKAS. Within these courses, I would bring open-source hardware for sound, including the Estuary instruments, into Sensors, Signals, and Interaction, and my teaching on AI as a cultural technology into Machine Collaboration. I look forward to joining the Teaching and Learning Center's Teaching Professors Learning Community and to sharing the online and access-centered practices I have developed with colleagues, as I have done as an invited speaker at SAIC's Center for Teaching and Learning (2025).


## Mentoring

I mentor students formally and informally, from first-year undergraduates to MFA candidates. Since 2021, I have held more than thirty individual studio visits with graduate students, in my seminar, across SAIC's Art and Technology Studies department, and beyond it; one student wrote that these visits make “the course feel grounded in your actual practice and not in just theory.” I am advising an MFA student on the literature review for a disability studies thesis on the aesthetics and perception of kinetic art, and I served on SAIC's Art and Technology Studies graduate admissions committee (2023–2024). In a one-semester independent study, I guided an undergraduate through designing her own research study, a mixed-methods proposal on how non-realistic environments shape communication in social VR, with a literature review, interview and focus-group protocols, and an observational study. I have written more than forty letters of recommendation.

These relationships have lasting results. Leonardo Gabriel do Amaral developed his interest in metamodernism through studio visits and assignments in my graduate seminar, where we connected it to our reading of Amy Ireland and Maya B. Kronic's *Cute Accelerationism*. I then worked with him on the essay that became “Reference vs Reverence: Post-hyperpop & the Rebirth of the Future” (*CLOT Magazine*, 2026). Lorenzo Osterheim took the same seminar, then served as my teaching assistant in Art and Technology Practices and in the seminar itself, and I hired him as an artist assistant for CCAM's 2025 exhibition *How to See in the Dark*. He and Ben Glass, also my former student, went on to found Flatline Gallery. The gallery takes its name from Mark Fisher's dissertation *Flatline Constructs*, which we discussed in the seminar, and continues to explore questions the seminar raised. Mentoring also continues outside degree programs. A student from my gen~ course at Experimental Sound Studio now develops and releases his own Max for Live devices built with gen~, and I am advising him on PCB and firmware design for a guitar pedal based on the Daisy Seed. Students I have recommended have gone on to graduate study, including Sao Otake in the master's program at NYU's Interactive Telecommunications Program (ITP) and Jude Silvertree, who is pursuing an MLIS focused on digital media at the University of Pittsburgh.

CCAM, the artist-run nonprofit I founded and direct in Chicago, extends this mentoring beyond the classroom. I commission artists to design and lead public workshops, and I mentor them as they plan and teach. Workshop leaders have included my former students Juliana Castro Duperly and Patrick Glennon, and another former student, Juan Flores, worked with me to develop firmware for CCAM Earth, a reprogrammable desktop synthesizer I developed, for our May 2024 program *Futurhythmachines: Chicago House Music*; all three were paid for their work. Many other former students have joined CCAM's gen~ working group. CCAM also sells Estuary modules at workshops and at Experimental Sound Studio's experimental garage sale, and I work with community members as they develop their own firmware for them. Earlier, I chaired the Doctoral Consortium of the International Conference on Movement and Computing (MOCO) in 2019 and directed ASU's Arts, Media and Engineering Graduate Student Association (2015–2018).

My mentoring rests on practices that research on mentoring supports. My mentees and I set shared expectations at the outset, meet on a regular schedule, and attend to the whole person, including the work, family, and financial pressures many students carry. I make the hidden curriculum explicit by teaching students how to document their work, write artist statements, respond to open calls, and apply for grants. I help each student build a network of several mentors rather than relying on me alone. Wherever I can, I turn mentoring into paid professional experience, as a teaching assistant, artist assistant, collaborator, or workshop leader. At UCSC, I would mentor Creative Technologies students through the Senior Studio (CT 195), supervise independent studies in sound and creative coding, and help students present their work on campus and beyond, including at conferences such as NIME and MOCO. I would also seek out formal mentor training to deepen this practice.


## Teaching and Mentoring Students from a Range of Social, Cultural, and Educational Backgrounds

Much of my teaching has served students for whom art and technology were not an obvious path, at three public universities. At Arizona State University, now a Hispanic-Serving Institution, I moved a section of my 100-seat Introduction to Digital Culture online midway through Spring 2020, when the pandemic closed campus. At SUNY Purchase, where about four in ten students are the first in their families to attend college, I taught Programming for Visual Arts online in Spring 2022. UIC, like UCSC, is a Hispanic-Serving Institution and an AANAPISI that enrolls many low-income and first-generation students. My Introduction to Creative Coding there fulfills a general education requirement. In Fall 2025, 78% of responding students took it for that reason, and most came from engineering and from liberal arts and sciences, with others from nursing, medicine, the honors college, and the arts; 44% were in their first year. They rated the course 4.67 out of 5 for being intellectually challenging and stimulating and 4.56 for increasing their interest in the subject. Four commitments guide my teaching across these differences.

**Lowering the cost of entry.** My introductory courses assume no prior programming and are built on free, browser-based tools such as the open-source p5.js and the New Art City platform, so no student has to buy software or a powerful computer to participate. In my SAIC creative coding course, five of six responding students reported spending nothing on required materials. When a student in an Arduino-based studio wrote that they could not afford materials beyond the required kit, I took it as a design problem. At UCSC, I would build lending kits and browser-based circuit simulation into physical computing courses so that cost never determines who gets to experiment.

**Designing for neurodiversity and disability.** My approach to access is grounded in research and in collaboration with educators. I co-founded and worked as a researcher on a National Science Foundation–funded research–practice partnership (2021–2025) that developed tangible, music-based computational thinking activities with teachers of neurodiverse middle school students. I have co-authored research on neurodiversity and schooling (*Reconceptualizing Educational Research Methodology*, 2022) and co-presented on wearable technologies for sensory regulation at the Arizona Council on Exceptional Children (2022). Following Erin Manning, who extends Whitehead's process philosophy to neurodiversity, I treat differences in attention, movement, and perception as resources for art-making rather than deficits to manage. In practice, I present every concept in several modes (live demonstration, recording, annotated example code, and embodied activity). I also offer several ways to participate, including speaking, chat, private messages, and asynchronous comments, and online students choose their own display names and camera settings. UIC students rated my sensitivity to “cultural/human diversity, diverse worldviews, learning disability, and/or physical disability” 5.0 (Fall 2024) and 4.88 (Fall 2025) out of 5.

**Making belonging part of the content.** I teach technology as culturally plural, pairing canonical figures in new media art with artists and traditions its histories often leave out. In my graduate seminar, students build a collaborative wiki that maps the course's concepts and connects them to cultures and ideas they already know, so that each student's background becomes part of the class's shared knowledge. SAIC's Center for Teaching and Learning funded the wiki and invited me to present its design in 2025. A graduate student wrote that when I brought the Chinese science fiction writer Liu Cixin into a seminar discussion, it “resonated with me and made me feel very happy,” noting that other courses sometimes overlook Chinese artists. Through CCAM, where I approach programming not as mere event planning but as the creative design of learning communities, I have co-organized three annual editions (2024–2026) of *We Don't Know Yet: What a Cinema Can Do*, an expanded cinema night with the Onion City Film Festival that centers BIPOC and queer artists, and *Futurhythmachines: Chicago House Music* (2024), a program of panels, workshops, and performances. At UCSC, I look forward to connecting creative coding and sound with the Music Department's strengths in African American and Global African musical traditions, Hip-Hop, Chicano music, and aural diversity. One example would be sampling and sequencing projects that study those traditions as sites of technological invention.

**Flexible structures, revised from evidence.** I design my courses so that a missed class or a hard week does not end a student's progress. I record every tutorial, in person and online, and post it to the course site, and students consistently name the recordings as essential (“The ZOOM recordings helped me a lot throughout the course”). Late coding sketches earn half credit for a week after their module ends, and late projects lose one letter grade per week rather than being refused. Every project prompt lists its requirements and submission items alongside the published rubric, so expectations never depend on knowing unwritten rules. I also revise in response to students. When a Fall 2022 student explained that having beginners teach programming fundamentals to one another early in the term caused confusion, I began opening with instructor-led, think-aloud tutorials and saving student-led teaching for a group presentation later in the term. In Fall 2025, a UIC student noted that the same few people did most of the talking in critique. My rubric already credits feedback given aloud, in chat, or in writing, and my next revision adds rotating critique roles so that every student has a way in. At UCSC, I would work with the Teaching and Learning Center to examine my course outcomes by student group and use anonymous mid-quarter check-ins to catch problems while they can still be fixed. Online classrooms are where I have done much of my best teaching, and I am committed to making Creative Technologies' online courses as rigorous, welcoming, and connected as any studio on campus.
